Printable markers

Twenty 9 cm markers, four to a sheet, plus four 5 cm spares. Each has an X whose crossing — the white dot — is the point you measure to and later click on. Print this page and they come out at true size, with a 10 mm gap and a dashed guide between neighbours to cut along.

Print at 100 % scale, portrait — not “fit to page”. Then hold a ruler against the 100 mm bar at the bottom of each sheet. If it isn’t exactly 100 mm, the print scaled, and that same percentage becomes a bias in every measurement you take today. The sheets will look completely normal either way, which is what makes this worth ten seconds.

Why 9 cm

It is tempting to size a marker so it covers several cells of the depth map. That is unreachable. The backbone works at 384×288, so the depth grid has an effective fx of only ≈ 89 px — even a 12 cm marker spans 2.7 depth cells at 4 m. You would need a half-metre board.

It doesn’t matter, because the marker is not the object being measured. It is a label saying “this pixel”, lying flat against a wall or box whose depth is what we actually want — and the depth behind the paper is the depth of the paper.

So the only real constraint is clicking, which happens on the full rectified 1640×1232 image where fx = 379.6 px. Pixels across = fx × size ÷ distance:

6 px is the floor for placing a click; the capture tool’s ×8 zoom makes 8 px comfortable. 9 cm clears the floor across the whole range a robot on a 3.66 m field can produce — and it is the largest size that fits four to a portrait A4 with a cutting gap, so twenty markers is five sheets instead of ten. At the far end, click the four corners instead of the centre: the tool intersects the diagonals, which is exact where averaging the corners is not.

Placing them

Keep each marker roughly a hand’s width clear of any edge, corner or doorway. That is the one case where the coarse depth grid does bite — a marker straddling a depth discontinuity gets foreground and background blurred together, and the number you record belongs to neither.

Spread them out. At least half should land outside the central ToF box, so the session measures extrapolation rather than just the region the sensor already covers.

Measuring, once they’re up

One straight-line measurement per marker, from the camera lens to the white dot — through the air, at whatever angle it runs. Not to the ToF sensor, which sits 20.2 mm lower. Not a floor or top-down distance: for a marker 1 m above the lens at 3 m ahead those differ by 162 mm, which is larger than the error being measured.
