Why the middle panel matters

The ToF covers a central box of roughly 315×447 pixels — 7 % of the frame. Everything else in panel 3 is a monocular network being stretched to fit those few hundred real measurements. That is the whole problem RingFusion exists to solve, and the reason every accuracy number here is scored on ToF zones the pipeline was not allowed to see.

The A/B: does the blend actually help while moving?

Both arms share one backbone and residual pass per frame, so every difference is the blend and nothing else. The blend was fed only the central 16×16 island; every scored zone lies outside it, so it never saw an answer.

The blend helps where it has anchors to work with and the two arms converge as the angle grows — by 30–90° they are identical to three decimals. That is the designed behaviour: it is a local correction, not a global one. It is not leaking near-field ToF depth out into the far field, which was the failure worth watching for.

What this run did not test

Every scored zone here is in the outer ring, so this measured extrapolation only. The blend's large win is on interpolation between zones inside the cone, where offline it takes MAE from 0.261 m to 0.045 m — and no zone inside the cone was scored. That benefit remains offline-only for now.

Two things offline could never test, both now measured: temporal stability is identical (median frame-to-frame change 2.7 mm blended against 2.8 mm not — the blend adds no flicker under motion), and the far field does not blow up (max depth 2.93 m blended against 3.08 m, nothing beyond the ToF range in either arm).

Getting there: 7.16 → 10.37 Hz

The first on-robot measurement failed its target. An extrapolation had predicted 9.9 Hz; the robot returned 7.2 Hz, below the 8.3 Hz rate at which the ToF itself delivers complete maps.

Queue depth 5→1, lazy rectify10.37Hz128msabove 8.3 Hz

The last row changed no arithmetic at all. Depth maps were arriving four frames behind reality because the subscription queues were five deep and the ToF outruns the pipeline — so the pipeline was working through a backlog of frames that were already obsolete. Setting the queues to one, and rectifying only the frame actually used rather than all thirty per second, cut staleness by two thirds with no change in throughput. That signature — latency moving while rate does not — is what identifies a queueing problem rather than a speed one.

The ground-plane RANSAC at the bottom — 0.10 ms — was 20 ms this morning and was the single largest component of the ROI stage. Vectorising it and moving the ROI mask onto the GPU is most of the rate gain above. There is no longer a stage whose cost is an artefact of how it was written: what remains is the two networks and the blend.

The trade, now that both sides are measured

The blend costs 15.35 ms of a 96 ms frame. Turning it off gives 12.4 Hz instead of 10.4 Hz — a 19 % rate gain — for 5.4 % worse MAE. Which side of that is right depends on what consumes the depth map, so it stays a parameter rather than a hard-coded choice: blend:=false.
